What to print on the care label of a grip sock

Grip socks generate one predictable complaint: the grip stopped working. Four things actually happen, and only one of them is a manufacturing fault — a care label is the cheapest way to stop the other three being blamed on the sock.

Softener leaves a film. Fabric conditioner is designed to coat fibre, and it coats silicone and PVC just as willingly. The dots are still there; the surface is now slippery. It is the most common cause of "the grip went soft", and it never shows up in a wash test unless someone adds softener on purpose.

Heat ages the polymer. A hot wash and a tumble dryer shorten the life of silicone, and shorten it faster on natural rubber grips, which yellow and crack as they oxidise. Oxygen bleach accelerates the same process. Grip socks want cool water and air drying — that is a fact about the material, not a brand preference.

Lint packs between the dots. This one is invisible, and it is what most wearers are describing when they say the grip has gone. Cotton fuzz, carpet fibre and machine debris fill the recesses between dots until the sole is a flat mat. Rub it off with a thumb and the grip comes back. One line of care instruction saves a complaint nobody can win.

The fourth is honest wear: the polymer abrades where you stand. Nothing prevents that except a taller dot at the print screen, and that is a specification decision, not a care one.

So what goes on the label? Five lines, and they fit on a small tag: machine wash cool at 30 degrees or lower; do not use fabric softener; do not tumble dry; wash inside out; do not bleach.

"Wash inside out" is the one most programmes skip, and it is the line that protects the print rather than the grip: turning the sock inside out stops the dots grinding against the rest of the wash.

Two notes for whoever writes the spec. Decide now whether the care text is sewn in, printed on the sole or carried on the hang tag — it changes packing and cost, and retrofitting a label after the first complaint is far more expensive than printing one. And if you sell in the EU, the fibre composition has to be on the textile label anyway under Regulation (EU) 1007/2011, so the care lines are a small addition to a tag you are producing regardless.
