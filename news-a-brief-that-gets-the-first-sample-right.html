A brief that gets the first sample right

Four things decide whether the first sample lands close or needs three rounds: artwork resolution, colour reference, size range, and construction notes.

Artwork should be vector, or raster at 300 dpi at final print size. A screenshot of a sock you liked is a fine starting point for a mock-up but not for production. Colour should be given as Pantone numbers; a photo of a sock introduces camera white balance and screen calibration into the conversation, and the argument that follows costs more time than the Pantone book.

Size range and target height decide the needle and the yarn count. A crew at 18 cm and a knee-high at 35 cm are different machines, different yarns and different prices.

Construction notes cover toe (hand-linked or flat-lock), heel (standard, Y-heel, terry), cushioning, cuff depth and packaging. Anything missing we will ask about before programming, because changing it after the sample means starting the sample again.

If the height is still open, fix it before the brief goes out rather than after the mock-up. A knee-high and a crew differ in yarn count and in what the machine does at the band, so a brief that says "socks" produces a sample that answers a different question. The six heights are the list to choose from.

The four things that decide the first sample

Everything else can be adjusted after a mock-up. These four cannot, because changing them restarts the sample.

A brief that comes back right the first time

Here is the shape of a brief that produces one sample rather than three. Fibre: combed cotton with elastane in the rib. Gauge: 168N, or a sock to match if you have one in hand. Height: crew, 18 cm from the middle of the knitted heel to the top of the cuff. Sizes: EU 39–42, a single band for the first run. Colours: two, with the Pantone references written out rather than photographed. Logo: knit-in on the cuff, one position. Artwork: vector file, already at final size. Packing: poly bag with a header card. Quantity: 300 pairs, target ship date six weeks out.

Every line in that list is a decision the machine will make anyway. Written down, they are decisions you make; left out, they are decisions whoever programmes the file makes for you, and you find out at the mock-up stage.

The two items that most often come back wrong are the ones easiest to skip. Artwork sent as a screenshot has to be redrawn, and a redraw has to be approved again — that is two round trips before anything is knitted. A colour given as a photograph of a sock carries the camera's white balance and the screen's calibration into the conversation, and the argument that follows costs more time than opening a Pantone book.

Height is the third. A crew at 18 cm and a knee-high at 35 cm are different yarn counts on different machines, so a brief that says "socks" produces a sample that answers a question nobody asked. If the height is still open when the brief goes out, the sample will settle it the expensive way.

None of this takes long. The sheet is nine sections, about five minutes, and it removes most of the reason a first sample misses.

What buyers get wrong in the first email

What file should I send?

Vector, or raster at 300 dpi at final print size. A screenshot of a sock you liked is fine for a mock-up but not for production.

Is a photo of a colour acceptable?

No. A photo brings camera white balance and screen calibration into the conversation; give Pantone numbers instead.

What if the height is not decided yet?

Fix it before the brief goes out. A knee-high and a crew differ in yarn count and at the band, so 'socks' produces a sample answering a different question.
