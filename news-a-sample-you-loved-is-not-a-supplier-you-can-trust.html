A sample you loved is not a supplier you can trust

The most useful sentence I have read in a sourcing forum came from a buyer answering what makes him trust an unfamiliar supplier: ordering a sample and being pleased with it is not an indicator that this is a good supplier. He is right, and the reason is structural.

A sample is made by the best person available, at a comfortable pace, with the machine set specifically for it. Bulk is made by whoever is on the night shift, on a schedule, alongside two other orders. Those are two different processes that happen to share a building.

So the sample tells you what the factory can do on a good day. What you need to know is what it does on a normal day, in month three, when the yarn lot has changed.

Ask which line runs your order, and how many other styles are on it. A factory that can answer this has a plan. A factory that says "we will arrange it" has a queue you are not controlling.

Ask for the inspection record from a previous order, not a blank template. A filled sheet with defect counts and a signature is evidence. An empty form with an AQL number on it is decoration.

Fix the specification in writing before the deposit. Yarn count, gauge, weight per pair, elastane percentage, toe closure, colour references. If your order is defined only by a sample sock and a photograph, the bulk has no standard to be measured against — which is exactly how "the bulk feels thinner than the sample" arguments begin.

Ask what happened the last time a defect was found. Not the policy. The instance: who paid, who replaced, how long it took.

The same principle works inside one order. Ask for photographs at three points — after knitting, after boarding, after packing — with the date visible. A factory that photographs its own floor has nothing to hide, and a trader cannot produce those photographs because it is not there to take them.

None of this is about distrust. It is about making the difference between a good day and a normal day visible before you have paid for either. The specification sheet is the cheapest version of that discipline.
