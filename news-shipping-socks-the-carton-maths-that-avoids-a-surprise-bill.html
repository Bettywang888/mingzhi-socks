Shipping socks: the carton maths that avoids a surprise bill

Socks are light and bulky, which sounds like good news for freight and usually is not. You pay for space, not mass, and the space is decided by the carton long before anyone looks at a container.

Here is the arithmetic, using a realistic carton rather than a round number. Say a carton holds 200 pairs of men's crew and measures 60 × 40 × 30 cm.

Volume: 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.072 cbm. Per pair that is 0.00036 cbm — a number worth writing down, because it converts any order into freight instantly. 50,000 pairs is 18 cbm. That is a part-load, not a container.

Container: a 40HQ offers roughly 68 cbm of usable space after pallets and dead air. At 0.072 cbm per carton that is about 940 cartons, or 188,000 pairs. A 20GP is roughly 28 cbm usable, so about 380 cartons.

Air freight: chargeable weight is the greater of actual and volumetric, and volumetric is length × width × height in centimetres divided by 6000. Our carton is 12 kg volumetric against roughly 10 kg actual, so air is billed on 12. Socks almost always ship on volume, not mass.

The example above is a crew carton. Knit the same order as a knee-high or a thigh-high and the box holds fewer pairs while weighing more: volume per pair climbs, so a container fills sooner, and mass per pair climbs, which is what air freight bills on once it overtakes volume. The six heights are an input to the calculation, not a detail after it.

Three things follow. Ask for carton dimensions and pairs per carton at the quotation stage, not at booking. Fix the tolerance — a carton that grows by 2 cm on each side is 18 percent more volume and nobody will mention it. And be sceptical of vacuum compression: it can cut volume by a fifth, and it also crushes the cuff, which is the one part of a sock that does not recover.

None of this is exotic. It is just cheaper to do before the goods are made than after.

Running the numbers for your own order

Four numbers convert any order into freight, and all four come from the carton rather than the container. Pairs per carton, carton dimensions, volume per pair, and the volumetric divisor your forwarder uses. With those, the rest is multiplication.

Work from the carton in the article above: 200 pairs, 60 × 40 × 30 cm, 0.072 cbm per carton, which is 0.00036 cbm per pair. Five thousand pairs is 1.8 cbm — a part-load that ships happily as LCL and does not justify a container. Twenty thousand pairs is 7.2 cbm, still well inside a 20GP's roughly 28 cbm of usable space. Fifty thousand pairs is 18 cbm and starts to look like a container decision rather than a freight decision.

The same arithmetic explains why a knee-high order behaves differently from a crew order at identical pair counts. Fewer pairs fit in the same box because each pair takes more volume, so volume per pair rises and the container fills sooner. Mass rises too, and mass is what air freight bills on once it overtakes volume. A quote that says "same price, different length" is not comparing the same shipment.

Two habits remove most of the surprise. Ask for carton dimensions and pairs per carton at the quotation stage, when there is still room to change the packing. And fix the tolerance in writing: a carton that grows by two centimetres on each side is eighteen percent more volume, and nobody will mention it at booking.

Scepticism about vacuum compression is also earned. It can cut volume by a fifth and it also crushes the cuff, which is one part of a sock that does not recover. The freight you save and the product you damage are on the same invoice.

Getting the carton right before booking

Do socks ship on weight or volume?

Almost always volume. They are light and bulky, so you are buying cubic metres rather than kilograms.

How many pairs fit in a 40HQ?

About 188,000 at 0.072 cbm per 200-pair carton — roughly 940 cartons in about 68 cbm usable.

When should I ask for carton dimensions?

At the quotation stage, not at booking. Carton size and pairs per carton set the freight, and neither appears on a per-pair price.
