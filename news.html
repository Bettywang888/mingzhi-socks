Notes from the factory floor.

Short pieces on sock construction, sizing, sampling and freight — written by the people who programme the machines, not by a marketing team.

04 Nov 2026Sourcing

What a sock factory should be able to show you before you pay a deposit

Every factory will send you a catalogue. Far fewer will send you documents, and the moment they hesitate is the most useful signal you will get in the whole process.

Ask for four things, in this order. A business licence, so you can see the registered name, the founding year and whether the scope actually covers textiles. A factory address that matches the one on the licence — not a trading office. Photographs or a video call of the machines that will run your order, with the model visible. And the test documents for the materials being used, in the name of whoever actually holds them.

That last one is where buyers get fooled, and usually not on purpose. A knitting factory does not dye its own yarn and often does not spin it. So the OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate you receive is normally held by the yarn mill or the dyehouse, not by the factory you are paying. That is the correct arrangement — what gets certified is the material, not the sewing line. But you should be told which is which, in writing, before the deposit.

We put ours on one page: two documents are ours — the CE / GPSD declaration and its test report — and two belong to our yarn and dyeing suppliers. The scans are the originals, unedited.

Two more questions worth asking, because they are cheap to answer and expensive to discover later: who owns the programme file if you move to another factory, and what happens to your leftover yarn after the run. A factory that answers both without flinching is usually also telling the truth about capacity.

None of this requires a compliance department. It requires a factory that is willing to be specific.

03 Nov 2026Costing

Why two factories quote the same sock at different weights

Send one design to three factories and the samples will come back at three different weights. Same drawing, same colours, same size — and one sock is 42 grams while another is 58. The difference is invisible in a photo and obvious on a scale, which is why the scale is the first thing a serious buyer asks about.

Four things move weight, and none of them are cosmetic.

Yarn count. A finer yarn knitted on more needles gives a smoother face and less mass per square centimetre. A coarser yarn on fewer needles gives a thicker, warmer, heavier sock with a more pronounced rib. Neither is better; they are different products that happen to share a drawing.

Structure. A terry loop inside the sole adds cushioning and weight without changing the outside at all. A plain knit on the same design can be 15 grams lighter per pair. If your sample brief says "cushioned" and theirs says "plain", you were never comparing the same sock.

Density. Loops per centimetre, and how tightly the yarn is pulled. A machine run faster produces more pairs per hour and a looser, lighter fabric — this is the single easiest place to cut cost without changing the yarn, and the hardest for a buyer to see.

Elastane. A cuff with 6 percent elastane weighs more and holds better than one with 2. On a 50 gram sock that is a small number of grams and a large difference in how it feels after ten washes.

So specify weight in the brief, not just the design. "Men's crew, 25–27 cm, 50 g per pair, plus or minus 3 g" is a sentence that makes three quotes comparable. Without it you are comparing three different socks.

The specification sheet has a line for it.

02 Nov 2026Shipping

Shipping socks: the carton maths that avoids a surprise bill

Socks are light and bulky, which sounds like good news for freight and usually is not. You pay for space, not mass, and the space is decided by the carton long before anyone looks at a container.

Here is the arithmetic, using a realistic carton rather than a round number. Say a carton holds 200 pairs of men's crew and measures 60 × 40 × 30 cm.

Volume: 0.6 × 0.4 × 0.3 = 0.072 cbm. Per pair that is 0.00036 cbm — a number worth writing down, because it converts any order into freight instantly. 50,000 pairs is 18 cbm. That is a part-load, not a container.

Container: a 40HQ offers roughly 68 cbm of usable space after pallets and dead air. At 0.072 cbm per carton that is about 940 cartons, or 188,000 pairs. A 20GP is roughly 28 cbm usable, so about 380 cartons.

Air freight: chargeable weight is the greater of actual and volumetric, and volumetric is length × width × height in centimetres divided by 6000. Our carton is 12 kg volumetric against roughly 10 kg actual, so air is billed on 12. Socks almost always ship on volume, not mass.

Three things follow. Ask for carton dimensions and pairs per carton at the quotation stage, not at booking. Fix the tolerance — a carton that grows by 2 cm on each side is 18 percent more volume and nobody will mention it. And be sceptical of vacuum compression: it can cut volume by a fifth, and it also crushes the cuff, which is the one part of a sock that does not recover.

None of this is exotic. It is just cheaper to do before the goods are made than after.

01 Nov 2026SizingHow to build a sock size chart for your market

Sock sizing is not shoe sizing, and this is where most first orders go wrong. A sock is knitted to a band of shoe sizes and held there by stretch. Get the band wrong and the same sock fits two markets badly.

Only one number is universal: foot length in centimetres. EU, UK, US and JP are local conventions laid on top of it. So build the chart from the foot outwards, then translate — never translate first and hope the centimetres follow.

Three decisions define the chart. How many bands you carry: three is the minimum, four is common in Germany and Japan, where tighter bands are expected. Where the breaks sit: put them where that market's shoe sizes break, not at a round centimetre. And what goes on the pack: printed sock size, shoe-size equivalent, or both.

For reference, these are the bands we most often knit to. They are indicative, not a standard — every market's retailers bracket differently, and your existing chart always wins.

23–25 cm
EU 36–38 · UK 3.5–5.5 · US 4.5–6.5 · JP 23–25

25–27 cm
EU 39–41 · UK 6–8 · US 7–9 · JP 25–27

27–29 cm
EU 42–44 · UK 8.5–10.5 · US 9.5–11.5 · JP 27–29

29–31 cm
EU 45–47 · UK 11–13 · US 12–14 · JP 29–31

The figures are men's sizes. Women's US runs about two numbers higher for the same foot, which is why a unisex sock usually has to print two scales on one pack.

The trap is assuming a US 9–11 is the same sock as an EU 43–46. It is close, but the edges do not line up, and the customer at the edge is the one who returns it. If you sell into two markets, print two charts or knit two bands.

One more thing decides comfort more than the chart: ease. A sock is knitted shorter than the foot it is meant to fit, and the stretch of the yarn bridges the gap. Knit it too tight and it compresses; too loose and it bunches at the ankle. That is a yarn and elastane question, not a sizing question — which is why we ask for the chart before the yarn.

The specification sheet has a line for the size range and a box for your chart. Send it as a file and we knit to it.

31 Oct 2026SamplingWhy a sample takes 3–5 days and not 3 weeks

A single sock comes off the machine in minutes. So why does a sample take days? Because almost none of the time is knitting.

Five things happen before the machine runs. Your artwork is checked for resolution and colour count. The design is converted into a knit programme — this is the slow part, and it is skilled work: six colours in a row, a logo placed on a curved surface, stitches that have to land where the eye expects them. Yarn is wound onto cones in the right sequence. The machine is stripped and re-set. Then one pair is knitted, linked, boarded and photographed.

A factory that takes three weeks is not knitting more slowly. It is queueing: your programme waits behind someone else's bulk order, and the gap between "we have your file" and "we have started" is where the weeks go.

Three things push a sample past five days, and none of them are the knitting.

Artwork that arrives as a screenshot rather than a vector file, so it has to be redrawn and then approved again. A colour that has to be dyed rather than pulled from stock — custom dyeing adds one to two weeks on its own, and no factory can compress that. And a design that needs more colours per row than the gauge can carry, which means a conversation, not a delay.

So the honest version of "3–5 days" is: 3–5 days once the artwork is approved and the colours are in stock. With dyed-to-Pantone yarns, count a week to a fortnight before knitting starts. Anyone who promises otherwise is either using stock colours without telling you, or guessing.

What removes most of the risk is approving a digital mock-up first. We send one within 24 hours of a complete brief; you approve colours and placement on screen, and the machine only runs once. That single step is usually the difference between one revision and four.

The brief is on the customization page as a downloadable sheet. Nine sections, about five minutes.

30 Oct 2026ConstructionCuff, heel and toe — the three places quality shows first

You cannot see construction in a product photo. You can see it after three months of wear, in exactly three places.

The cuff. A cuff that sags is a cuff with the wrong elastane, or too little of it. Good cuffs carry 4–8 percent elastane in the rib and are knitted in a structure that grips without pinching. The test is physical: stretch the cuff to twice its width, hold for ten seconds, release, and measure. It should come back to within a few millimetres. If it stays stretched, so will the sock.

The heel. It is the first place to wear through, because it takes the abrasion and the shape change at the same time. The difference between a heel that lasts a season and one that lasts two is usually nylon: an extra ply in the heel, or a terry loop rather than a plain knit. Ask what is in the heel, not whether it is "reinforced" — reinforced is a word, nylon content is a number.

The toe. Turn the sock inside out and look at the seam. An overlocked seam is fast, bulky and the thing people feel first. A flat-lock seam is flatter and faster than hand linking. Hand-linked toes — the Rosso method — are closed stitch by stitch, which is why they feel like nothing at all, and why they cost more and take longer. If a supplier says "seamless toe", ask which of the three they mean.

None of this shows up in a photo, and all of it shows up in a review six months later. The cheapest way to check before ordering 2,000 pairs is to ask for a wash test: five cycles at 40 degrees, then look at the cuff and the heel again.

We knit all three methods. Which one is right depends on where the sock is sold and what it has to survive — send us the use case and we will say which one we would put on the machine.

29 Oct 2026CostingWhy the quote is a range, not a price per pair

Ask five sock factories for a unit price and you will get five numbers that cannot be compared, because none of them describe the same sock. Yarn weight, needle gauge, number of colours, number of logo positions, packing and order size all move the cost, and they move it by different amounts.

The biggest lever is not the yarn. It is machine time per pair. A 200-needle jacquard with six colours and a knitted-in logo runs far slower than a plain 144-needle crew, and that difference shows up in the price long before the cotton does. The second lever is order size: programming, winding the colours and knitting the first pair are fixed costs, so 100 pairs carries them heavily and 2,000 pairs barely notices them.

That is why a range is the honest answer at the enquiry stage. Once the quantity, the colour count and the logo method are fixed, the range narrows to a number - and that number is what goes in the quotation, with the trade term beside it.

If a factory gives you an exact price before asking any of these questions, the price is a guess dressed up as a fact.

28 Oct 2026Construction144N, 168N, 200N — what the needle count changes

The number on a sock machine is needles per 3.5 inches of cylinder. More needles means more, finer columns of stitches, which means a thinner yarn, a smoother surface and a design that can hold finer detail. Fewer needles means a coarser, thicker, warmer sock.

144N is the workhorse. It takes thicker yarn, runs fast, and suits wool blends, heavy crews and anything where hand-feel matters more than resolution. 168N is the middle: casual and sport styles where you want some structure without losing softness. 200N runs fine yarn and is where photographic jacquard, tight logos and dress socks live.

The practical consequence is design resolution. A six-colour logo with small lettering will collapse into a blur on 144N and read cleanly on 200N. A chunky boot sock knitted on 200N becomes thin and loses the point of being a boot sock.

If you are unsure, send the artwork and the intended use. The gauge usually chooses itself.

27 Oct 2026ConstructionJacquard, embroidery, print or grip — which one holds your design?

Jacquard knits the colour into the fabric. It is the only one of the four that cannot crack, peel or wash off, because there is no layer sitting on top of the yarn. The trade-off is colour count: each additional colour in the same row needs another feeder, so knit-in artwork normally stays around six colours per row. Stripes, geometric repeats, club colours and knit-in lettering are all ideal.

Embroidery adds a raised thread on top of a finished sock. It is the most expensive of the four per logo, and it is the one buyers choose when the sock has to read as retail rather than giveaway — a crest, a wordmark, a small badge on the cuff.

Printing and sublimation carry unlimited colour, gradients and photographic detail wrapped around the whole sock. Sublimation dyes polyester fibre itself, so it does not sit on the surface; on cotton the same artwork goes through heat transfer or screen print instead.

Grip is applied after knitting — silicone, PVC or flocked — as a full sole, heel-to-toe strips or a repeating dot pattern. It is what turns a training sock into a pilates, barre or trampoline product.

Send the artwork and we will tell you which of the four holds the detail you need. The answer is often a combination: knit-in body, embroidered logo, grip sole.

24 Oct 2026OrderingA spec sheet that gets you a real quote in one reply

Most first enquiries arrive as a photo and one line: "how much for these?" That starts a conversation of ten messages before anyone can quote, and every round trip costs a day.

The nine things a factory actually needs are: fibre, gauge (or a sock to match), height, size range, colour count with Pantone references, logo method and placement, artwork file, packing, and the first order quantity with a target ship date. None of them are difficult to answer, and all of them change the price.

We put them on a two-page sheet. Fill it in, send it with the logo file, and the reply is a price band, three yarn options and a mock-up date - not another question. The sheet also doubles as an internal document: if two people in your team fill it in differently, that disagreement is worth finding before the sample, not after.

Download it from the customization page. It takes about five minutes to complete and it saves roughly a week of email.

20 Oct 2026OrderingWhat 100 pairs actually covers

The minimum is 100 pairs per style, per colourway. Within that 100 you split the sizes however you need them — 25/25/25/25 across four sizes is a normal first order, and so is a single size for a team.

Below 100 the cost per pair climbs quickly, and not because of the yarn. Programming the machine, winding the colours and knitting the first pair are fixed costs that get divided by the order quantity. At 100 pairs they are noticeable; at 1,000 they are almost invisible.

That is why a first range is usually tested at 100 pairs in one or two colourways rather than 400 pairs in eight. Sell through the first drop, then reorder the winners — reorders skip programming entirely and are faster and cheaper than the first run.

If you are unsure which sizes to split, send your market and channel and we will suggest a ratio from previous orders.

06 Oct 2026SamplingA brief that gets the first sample right

Four things decide whether the first sample lands close or needs three rounds: artwork resolution, colour reference, size range, and construction notes.

Artwork should be vector, or raster at 300 dpi at final print size. A screenshot of a sock you liked is a fine starting point for a mock-up but not for production. Colour should be given as Pantone numbers; a photo of a sock introduces camera white balance and screen calibration into the conversation, and the argument that follows costs more time than the Pantone book.

Size range and target height decide the needle and the yarn count. A crew at 12 cm and a knee-high at 45 cm are different machines, different yarns and different prices.

Construction notes cover toe (hand-linked or flat-lock), heel (standard, Y-heel, terry), cushioning, cuff depth and packaging. Anything missing we will ask about before programming, because changing it after the sample means starting the sample again.
