Sampling fees: whether and when they are refunded

The sample fee is the first place a sourcing relationship shows whether it is straight, because it is small enough to argue about and large enough to reveal how a factory values its own time.

What the fee pays for is mostly not the sock. One pair costs very little in yarn. The fee covers the parts that do not scale down: converting artwork into a knit programme, winding the colours onto cones, stripping and re-setting the machine, knitting and linking the pair, boarding, photographing, couriering. The programming is skilled work, and it is the reason a fee exists at all.

Three arrangements are common, and all three are legitimate. Fee charged, credited against the bulk order: you pay at sample stage and the amount comes off the invoice when the order is placed — the fairest form, because the buyer who orders does not pay twice. Fee charged, not credited, which is defensible as long as it is stated, since the programming time is spent whether or not you order. No fee, priced into the bulk, where the programming is amortised into the unit price instead; if you never order, the factory absorbs it, which is why a free sample is normally offered on stock colourways rather than on your design.

A genuinely free custom sample is a warning rather than a gift. Either it is not custom, or the cost has moved somewhere else in the quote.

Four questions before paying, and they take one message to answer. Is the fee credited, and against what — any order, or only above a certain quantity? Does it cover one sample or revisions, and how many? Who pays the courier and the import duty on the sample, because the duty is frequently more than the freight and it is billed to whoever is the importer of record? And if the design changes materially after the sample, is that a revision or a new sample?

That last one causes most of the friction. "One revision included" normally means colour and placement adjustments; a new size, a different yarn or different artwork is a new programme, and a reasonable factory says so before starting rather than after.

Our own terms — the fee, the credit condition and what counts as a revision — are written into the quotation line by line, because a sample fee negotiated after the fact is a poor way to begin. If a quote you receive does not state those three things, ask, and treat a vague answer as information about how the rest of the order will run.

The fastest way to make the fee worth it is to get the first sample right: the specification sheet takes five minutes, and a sample that lands first time is cheaper than any fee.
