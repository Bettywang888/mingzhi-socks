What a sock factory should be able to show you before you pay a deposit

Every factory will send you a catalogue. Far fewer will send you documents, and the moment they hesitate is the most useful signal you will get in the whole process.

Ask for four things, in this order. A business licence, so you can see the registered name, the founding year and whether the scope actually covers textiles. A factory address that matches the one on the licence — not a trading office. Photographs or a video call of the machines that will run your order, with the model visible. And the test documents for the materials being used, in the name of whoever actually holds them.

That last one is where buyers get fooled, and usually not on purpose. A knitting factory does not dye its own yarn and often does not spin it. So the OEKO-TEX Standard 100 certificate you receive is normally held by the yarn mill or the dyehouse, not by the factory you are paying. That is the correct arrangement — what gets certified is the material, not the sewing line. But you should be told which is which, in writing, before the deposit.

We put ours on one page: two documents are ours — the CE / GPSD declaration and its test report — and two belong to our yarn and dyeing suppliers. The scans are the originals, unedited.

Two more questions worth asking, because they are cheap to answer and expensive to discover later: who owns the programme file if you move to another factory, and what happens to your leftover yarn after the run. A factory that answers both without flinching is usually also telling the truth about capacity.

Ask a fifth thing while the documents are out: which heights the floor actually runs. A factory that lists a thigh-high but cannot show you one has not made one, and the height is the first thing that decides whether your sample is possible at all. The two long lengths are what we run, with the band decision each one forces.

None of this requires a compliance department. It requires a factory that is willing to be specific.

The five-minute document check

The check is quick if you know what each document is supposed to prove. On the business licence, read the registered name, the founding year, and whether the stated scope actually covers textiles — a scope that stops at trading is worth knowing about before the deposit rather than after. Then compare the factory address on the licence with the address you are being shown; a mismatch is the single cheapest signal available.

On the machine evidence, look for the model rather than the floor. A photograph of a knitting hall proves a knitting hall exists; a photograph where the cylinder and the model number are legible proves which machine will run your order. On a video call, ask to see the cylinder and the feeders, and ask how many needles — those are answers a sales office cannot improvise.

On the material test documents, the question is ownership rather than authenticity. OEKO-TEX Standard 100 is normally held by the yarn mill or the dyehouse, because what gets certified is the material rather than the sewing line. A knitting factory that dyes nothing cannot hold that certificate in its own name, and a factory that says otherwise is either confused or hoping you are. What should be in the factory's own name is the CE / GPSD declaration and its test report, which cover the product being placed on the market.

Two dates decide whether any of it is current. A scope certificate expires annually, so a scan without a visible validity period is not evidence of anything. A test report older than the material specification it covers is equally weak.

Ask the two uncomfortable questions while the documents are out, because they are cheap now and expensive later: who owns the programme file if you move to another factory, and what happens to your leftover yarn after the run. A factory that answers both without hesitating is usually also telling the truth about capacity.

Reading a certificate correctly

Why is the OEKO-TEX certificate not in the factory's name?

Because what gets certified is the material, not the sewing line. The certificate is normally held by the yarn mill or the dyehouse.

Does that mean something is wrong?

No, it is the correct arrangement. What matters is being told which document is whose, in writing, before the deposit.

What should the factory address match?

The address on the business licence. A trading office address is the signal worth noticing.
