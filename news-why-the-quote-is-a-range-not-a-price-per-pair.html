Why the quote is a range, not a price per pair

Ask five sock factories for a unit price and you will get five numbers that cannot be compared, because none of them describe the same sock. Yarn weight, needle gauge, number of colours, number of logo positions, packing and order size all move the cost, and they move it by different amounts.

The biggest lever is not the yarn. It is machine time per pair. A 200-needle jacquard with six colours and a knitted-in logo runs far slower than a plain 144-needle crew, and that difference shows up in the price long before the cotton does. The second lever is order size: programming, winding the colours and knitting the first pair are fixed costs, so 100 pairs carries them heavily and 2,000 pairs barely notices them.

That is why a range is the honest answer at the enquiry stage. Once the quantity, the colour count and the logo method are fixed, the range narrows to a number - and that number is what goes in the quotation, with the trade term beside it.

We write the band out rather than hide it. What our price is not — not the lowest you will be quoted, not one number off a list, and not delivered — sits on the contact page before you send anything. It takes a minute to read and saves the three emails it would otherwise take to establish the same thing.

Height is the lever buyers forget. The same design on a knee-high wears more yarn and holds the cylinder longer than on a crew, so the pair costs more before any artwork is added — the two long lengths are where that shows up hardest.

Collapsing the range in one message

A range collapses to a number when the levers stop moving. In practice that means five answers: quantity per style and colourway, colour count per row, logo method and how many positions, height with a measurement, and packing. Send those and the reply stops being a band.

The quantity answer matters more than buyers expect, and it cuts both ways. At 100 pairs per colourway the fixed work — programming, winding, the first pair — is a visible share of every pair. At 2,000 it is almost invisible. That is not a discount policy; it is the same cost divided by a larger number, and it is why a range is the honest answer at the enquiry stage rather than a sales tactic.

Height belongs in the message because it is the lever most often forgotten. The same design on a knee-high takes more yarn and holds the cylinder longer than on a crew, so it costs more before any artwork is added, and the two long lengths are where that shows up hardest. A quote that does not name the height is not a comparable quote.

The last item is the trade term. A pair price without EXW, FOB, CIF or DDP beside it cannot be compared with another quote, because the numbers describe different journeys. FOB is the one that makes two quotes comparable, so ask for it even when you intend to buy on another term.

If a factory gives you an exact number before asking any of this, it is a guess wearing a price tag. The useful reply is a range with its assumptions written out — and once the assumptions are fixed, the range closes.
